Fluid Mechanics, Dynamical Systems, Statistical Mechanics, Machine Learning

Currently a 2nd year PhD student in Environmental Fluid Mechanics at Stanford University, with an MS in Computational and Mathematical Engineering from Stanford (2026) and a BA in Physics from Wesleyan University (2024). After a mix of computational neuroscience and experimental fluid mechanics as an undergraduate, I am now focused on developing theory to describe multi-scale fluid systems.

Neural networks are efficient high dimensional function approximators which motivate the classification of problems as "data sparse" or "data rich". Traditional methods for developing physical theories, by perturbing conservation laws, are most relevant in the data sparse regime. Centuries of clever tricks have been developed to approach data sparse problems, but only recently has equivalent progress been made in the data rich regime. I have found both approaches, and the problems they are best suited for, fascinating and am working to develop and apply methods in both regimes.

I am especially excited about research that invites new physical systems into the partial differential equations family, typically by a process known as "coarse-graining". If your system behaves unusually smoothly when you squint at it from far away, let me know! There's likely a PDE hiding somewhere nearby.

Data-Sparse Science

Statistical Theory for the Microscopic Ocean

What does the ocean look like on scales of 1 micron to 1 centimeter? Here, I am developing a quantitative theory to account for the effect of microorganisms swimming and dying in generating sustained microscopic fluctuations. Specifically, I am interested in the balance of turbulent mixing, which homogenizes the small scales, and the release of chemical potential, which can produce and correlate fluctuations in nutrient/organism concentration fields.

Effects of Mirror Symmetry Breaking on Particle-Fluid Interactions

How does a particle's shape determine the pattern it traces as it falls? Here we show that particles which differ from their mirror image (like a propeller or a spiral) can trace complex trajectories due to their broken symmetry. We use symmetry arguments to constrain the nonlinear dynamics and eventually produce a method to categorize any shape by its behavior as it falls.

Advection Heads in an Atmosphere Foundation Model

Advection describes the transport of information by a velocity field, and is a fundamental process in flow physics. We study this process in Aurora, a foundation model for atmospheric dynamics, and discover "advection heads" which appear to move information along flow lines and in some sense play a similar role to induction heads in LLMs.
