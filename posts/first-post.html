Coarsegraining to Build Simple Models

August 2, 2026

Burger's equation is arguably the simplest nonlinear partial differential equation and is often used as a stepping stone on the way to more difficult PDEs. Most notably, the system can develop shocks where the velocity field becomes multivalued, and motivates the introduction of "weak formulations" which can handle non-smooth solutions.

Here, I will instead use the Burger's equation to introduce coarse-graining, a method to turn the equations of motion of a particle into those of a field. Approaching this problem from the perspective of a particle makes some of the counterintuitive aspects of the PDE problem feel physically obvious.

Consider a particle with initial position $x_i(0)$ and velocity $v_i(0)$, with the index $i$ denoting the vector component, whose dynamics are governed by $$ \frac{dV_{i}(t)}{dt} = 0 \quad \frac{dX_{i}(t)}{dt} = V_{i}(t)= V_{i}(0) $$ we can define a velocity field, some $u_i(x,t)$ which has a vector value at every position and time, and constrain it to match the particle's velocity when their positions and times coincide $$ u_i(X(t),t) = \frac{dX_{i}(t)}{dt} = V_{i}(0)$$ We can derive the evolution equation for $u_i$ by differentiating and using the chain rule, $$ \frac{d}{dt} u_i(X(t),t) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}u_i + \frac{dX_{j}(t)}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} u_i = 0$$ which can be simplified using the constraint on $u_i(x,t)$ to get$$\partial_t u_i + u_j\partial_ju_i = 0 $$The partial differential equation, called Burger's Equation, above is valid for any position and time $(x,t)$ occupied by exactly one particle. If we release a single particle, then the equation is valid at all times, but vanishingly few positions. If we release a particle from every point in the domain, the equation is valid at every position, but up until the particles collide and $u(x,t)$ becomes multivalued (when one particle catches up to its neighbor with a different velocity).

Physically, we've discovered that when two particles occupy the same $(x,t)$, no interaction is short or weak enough to be neglected. To resolve the collision problem, we must specify new information about the particles. In a pure PDEs approach, this is the point at which a variational principle is used to select the correct solution, but from a particle perspective we are simply choosing an interaction rule (to me at least, this is significantly more intuitive).
